Chunking: Cutting Documents Into Useful Pieces

Before anything can be embedded or searched, a document must be split into chunks — small pieces of text that each carry one coherent idea.

Why chunk at all?

Embedding models have a practical size limit (a few hundred to a few thousand tokens), and search quality collapses when a chunk tries to cover too much: a chunk containing ten topics matches ten different questions badly, because its meaning is muddy. Small, focused chunks match well.

The three settings that matter

  1. Chunk size. How many characters (or tokens) per chunk. Rules of thumb:
  • 200–400 characters: fast, precise, may lose context
  • 800–1200 characters: the sweet spot for most prose
  • 2000+ characters: only for long, uniform documents
  1. Overlap. How much consecutive chunks share, usually 10–20% of size. Overlap prevents a sentence that straddles a boundary from being cut in half and lost. Without overlap, "the answer was in the last chunk's first line" chunks fail to retrieve.
  2. Splitting strategy. Where to cut:
  • Naive: fixed windows of N characters. Fast, but cuts mid-sentence.
  • Recursive: try to split on paragraphs, then sentences, then words — only falling back to raw characters if a piece is still too big. This is what most production systems (and LangChain) do, and it is what our example uses.

A mental model

Think of a book being turned into index cards. Each card should hold one paragraph of one point with its context. The "context" part is why overlap exists: if a paragraph begins "Here's why that fails…", the card is useless unless the card before it (or an overlap sentence) explains what "that" is.

Worked example

Given text of 10,000 characters with size=800, overlap=100:

  • Chunk 1: characters 0–799
  • Chunk 2: characters 700–1499 (100 chars overlap)
  • Chunk 3: characters 1400–2199
  • … and so on, stepping 700 each time.

The recursive splitter changes where the cuts happen (sentence and paragraph boundaries) but not how much is covered.

Pitfalls

  • Headers mean a fresh topic — always start a new chunk at a heading.
  • Tables and code should not be split mid-structure; treat them as atomic chunks if smaller than the limit.
  • Too-small chunks retrieve precise-looking sentences with no surrounding meaning; too-large chunks drown the key sentence in noise.
  • No metadata — always attach the source filename and chunk position to every chunk now; you'll need it for citations in Chapter 07.

Try it

The chunker in examples/01-index.py implements recursive splitting with overlap and metadata. Run it on this manual itself:


python3 examples/01-index.py docs --index /tmp/opencode/demo-index.json

Then inspect /tmp/opencode/demo-index.json — you'll see exactly where the cuts landed and why.
